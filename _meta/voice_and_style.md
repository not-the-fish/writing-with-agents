# Voice and Style

A living profile of the writer's voice, style, and craft. Co-developed with agents through close reading of existing work.

*Seeded from initial read of full corpus (April 2025). Writer should correct, expand, and push back.*

## Influences

**Julia Evans** — Accessible technical explainers with genuine enthusiasm. Evans makes complex systems (DNS, networking, bash) feel approachable without ever condescending. Her tone is curious and delighted rather than authoritative. Connection to existing work: the academic blog posts share this instinct — making methodology fun, bringing readers along. Evans is a model for the newsletter voice the writer wants to develop: technical precision with warmth and personality.

**Ed Zitron** — Combative, deeply reported tech criticism with a strong moral core. Zitron's newsletter voice is direct, angry when warranted, funny, and refuses to both-sides things that have a clear right answer. Connection to aspirations: the writer wants more distinguishing perspective and brand in professional writing. Zitron models what it looks like to have a point of view and not hedge it. The newsletter draft already has flashes of this ("I'm not going to romanticize these jobs") but could push further.

**Kazuo Ishiguro** — Prose style, wry humor, and above all, the obsession with how we tell our own stories. Ishiguro interrogates the ways people protect themselves from painful self-examination — his characters narrate around the wound, and the reader sees what the narrator can't or won't. The writer shares this obsession with self-reflection but approaches it from the opposite direction: where Ishiguro's characters fail at self-examination, this writer charges toward it, sometimes painfully. They are two sides of the same preoccupation — Ishiguro on how we fail to see ourselves, and this writer on trying to overcome that failure. The aspiration is not to write like Ishiguro but to learn from his control: how much can be conveyed through structure and indirection rather than direct statement.

**Melissa Febos** (*Girlhood*) — One of the writer's favorite essay collections. Febos models what it looks like to write personal essays that are simultaneously intimate and intellectually rigorous, where the autobiographical material serves a larger argument without losing its emotional specificity. A reference point for the creative nonfiction voice the writer is developing.

**Patrick Radden Keefe** — Narrative nonfiction that combines deep reporting with compelling storytelling. Keefe demonstrates how to handle complex, morally ambiguous material with both rigor and momentum — a model for how research and narrative can reinforce each other rather than competing. Crucially, Keefe sustains tension across long pieces without exhausting the reader. His prose is "more-ish": the lyricism and narrative propulsion make you want to keep going rather than needing a break. This is a quality the writer wants to develop, particularly for the longer personal and professional pieces where reader fatigue is a risk.

**Tangle (Isaac Saul)** — Depth of analysis combined with genuine engagement with opposing perspectives. What the writer admires: the ability to understand others' positions thoroughly and still stand firmly by your own. Relevant to the newsletter aspiration — Tangle models a professional voice that is fair-minded without being wishy-washy.

**Benn Stancil** — Opinionated, clear-eyed data writing with personality. Stancil's newsletter stands out in the data space for the strength of its convictions — he has a point of view and commits to it without hedging, but pairs that confidence with a willingness to admit when he's wrong or doesn't know. His expertise is evident not because he performs authority but because it's embedded in how he thinks through problems. The writing is genuinely creative and funny, which is rare in a space that tends toward the dry or the self-serious. Connection to aspirations: Stancil models the professional voice the writer is working toward — technically credible, distinctive, unafraid of strong opinions, and human enough to be uncertain. He demonstrates that humor and conviction can coexist with intellectual honesty in data writing.

**Karmela Padavic-Callaghan** — Prose beauty. The writer is drawn to the quality of the sentences themselves. An aspirational reference for the craft of figurative writing and the sentence-level music the writer wants to develop.

**Cormac McCarthy** — Relentless depth and attention to sound. McCarthy takes a thing and pushes it as far as it will go — an image, a landscape, a philosophical thread — without flinching or cutting away early. The writer admires this willingness to stay with something past the point of comfort, to keep pressing. Not the brutality, but the commitment. McCarthy is also a model for prose as sound: his sentences are built for the ear, with rhythms and cadences that carry meaning independent of content. Like Keefe, McCarthy sustains tension through lyricism — the prose itself generates momentum that makes the depth feel compelling rather than exhausting. Paired with Padavic-Callaghan, this points to an aspiration around the acoustic properties of prose — writing that rewards being read aloud and that carries the reader through even the longest, deepest passes.

**Will Schwalbe** — Vulnerability and the unflattering self-portrait in service of understanding relationships. Schwalbe's willingness to present himself as flawed — not for self-flagellation but because the real texture of a relationship requires showing where you fell short — is a direct model for the memoir work. The writer's memoir ideas (the Paula friendship, the evolving relationship with Erik) are built from the same material: moments where the writer was wrong, selfish, avoidant, or confused, presented honestly because that's where the emotional truth of the relationship lives. Schwalbe demonstrates that obsessive attention to a relationship's dynamics — the power shifts, the misreadings, the repair attempts — can carry an entire book.

## Signature Moves

**Extended technical metaphor as structural backbone.** The gender essay uses HTTP status codes (404, 200, 400) not as decoration but as the organizing architecture of the entire piece. The metaphor deepens and shifts meaning across the essay — 404 is loss, then defiance, then resignation; 200 is pretending; 400 is reclamation. This is the most distinctive craft move in the corpus and suggests a broader instinct: using systems and protocols as lenses for human experience.

**Parenthetical asides that carry the real payload.** Heavy use of parentheticals for humor, self-correction, and emotional commentary. These often do the most interesting work in a sentence: "(Really, Miranda, no one wants to click through 200 photos from your trip to Des Moines)," "(Of course, that was a lie I was telling myself. I'm a lot like both of them.)," "(hey, he won a Nobel Prize for it, and there isn't even a Nobel for psychology!)." The parenthetical is where the writer's personality lives most freely — it's the aside-as-voice.

**Self-interrogation as engine.** The writing advances by asking questions and working through them rather than declaring positions. "Was it in my heart? Definitely not. Was it in my skeleton? I don't think so." "But am I really right all the time?" "What alternatives remain? And how does one go about finding them?" This shows up everywhere — personal essay, academic blog, newsletter. The writer thinks by questioning rather than asserting.

**The honest admission as pivot point.** Multiple pieces hinge on a moment where the writer names something uncomfortable about themselves. "I was what is now called — but didn't have a name then — a 'pick-me girl.'" "I've made such inferences myself." "I'm not going to romanticize these jobs." These aren't confessions for their own sake — they're structural, creating turns in the argument or narrative. Trust is built by going first.

**Concrete specifics over abstractions.** The writing trusts the reader to generalize from particulars rather than the reverse. The gender essay piles up specific cultural artifacts (MySpace, Friendster, LiveJournal, DeadJournal, SNL's Pat, AIM, webrings). The newsletter names real organizations, real salary numbers, real job boards. The memoir ideas are all specific scenes and dialogue. Abstractions arrive late, earned by the specifics that precede them.

**Temporal scaffolding.** Time is a deliberate structuring device in the personal writing — "In 2004," "By the time Tumblr was created in 2007," "In the 20 years since" — and the movement through time often carries the emotional arc. The newsletter does this more subtly (tracing how the job market shifted). The writer thinks historically.

## Tonal Range

**Personal essay:** Literary, vulnerable, wry, and layered. Uses humor to manage pain and wit to maintain composure while discussing difficult material. The voice is intimate and confessional but also intellectually precise — the HTTP metaphor demands technical accuracy alongside emotional honesty. There is a refusal to simplify. The sentence-level voice here is the most distinctive and accomplished in the corpus.

**Newsletter:** Authoritative, frank, pragmatic. Speaks from experience without posturing. The tone is a knowledgeable colleague who has been through the thing and will give it to you straight — no false optimism, no condescension. Less playful than the personal writing but still has personality. There's a directness here ("I'm not going to romanticize these jobs") that reads as genuine rather than performed.

**Academic blog:** Nerdy-playful. "Holy hit rate, Batman!" "BOOM!! Batting a thousand!" Uses pop culture references (Taylor Swift vs. Baz Luhrmann sunscreen song) to make statistical concepts tangible. Genuinely enthusiastic about methodology. More performatively casual than the newsletter — this is someone who enjoys making statistics fun and wants to bring readers along for the ride. The longer analytical posts (Article Level Metrics, Grappling with the Past) shift into a more measured, careful register — still accessible but notably more hedged and precise.

**Love letter:** Warm, earnest, direct. Simple sentence structures. No cleverness, no intellectual scaffolding, no deflection. This is the only piece in the corpus that is purely emotional without any analytical frame, and it works precisely because of that restraint. The plainness reads as sincere. Notably shorter than everything else.

**Memoir notes/scenes:** Raw and scene-driven. The dialogue transcripts (Hook Erika Up Contest AIM logs, the memoir bullet points) reveal a sharp ear for how people actually talk and a documentary instinct — capture what happened, in order, with specificity. The notes are terse, almost journalistic. The drafted scenes are warmer and more narratively shaped but still grounded in dialogue and action over interiority.

**Business copy:** Professional, credential-forward, clean. Longer sentences, more institutional language, less personality than any other mode. The competence is visible but the person is partially hidden. This is the most conventional register in the corpus.

## Vocabulary Tendencies

**Technical vocabulary used casually:** "enshittification," "p-hacking," "publication bias," "digital Taylorism," "Rube Goldberg machine," "acyclic pipelines." The writer assumes a reader who can handle jargon from multiple domains (tech, statistics, web infrastructure) or who will follow context clues. There's rarely a "for those who don't know" frame — the reader is trusted.

**Pop culture and internet culture as shared language:** SNL's Pat, Taylor Swift, Baz Luhrmann, DeadJournal, webrings, under-construction gifs, animated cursors, Magic: The Gathering, LAN parties. These aren't name-drops; they're used for their connotative precision. The cultural references are millennial-internet-native and tend toward the slightly obscure or nostalgic.

**Colloquial interjections for pacing and personality:** "Gosh," "Dang," "Oops," "BOOM!!", "Holy hit rate, Batman!", "Hah! Joke's on you!" These are more frequent in the academic blog than elsewhere and serve as pressure valves in dense material.

**Em dashes for mid-sentence pivots:** Used heavily across all registers. The writer thinks in clauses and qualifications, and the em dash is the primary tool for managing that complexity. Sometimes three or four in a single sentence.

**Repetition as rhetorical device:** "Not Found" recurs throughout the gender essay as a refrain, gaining weight and shifting meaning with each appearance. The p-hacking post builds through repeated simulation results. The writer is comfortable with (and skilled at) deliberate repetition.

**Academic hedging that leaks into other registers:** "I suspect that," "it's not clear that," "at least some of the time," "it remains possible that." This caution is earned in the academic context but occasionally dampens the personal and professional writing where more assertion would serve the piece.

## Recurring Themes

**The failure of categories.** The gender essay is about not fitting into binary categories. The academic work interrogates when statistical categories (significant/not significant) fail to capture reality. The newsletter wrestles with the category of "good work" vs. "tech work." There is a deep, consistent skepticism toward systems that sort people into boxes — and a fascination with what falls through.

**How systems serve and fail people.** HTTP protocols, statistical methods, the academic publishing system, the tech job market, social media platforms, healthcare intake forms — the writer is drawn to understanding how systems work and documenting the moments when they don't serve the people they're supposed to. The systems are never dismissed; they're interrogated with the seriousness of someone who understands and respects them.

**The gap between how you're seen and who you are.** The gender essay is centrally about this. The impostor syndrome flowchart is literally about this. The business bio presents a curated professional self that omits everything the personal writing reveals. The writer is preoccupied with the distance between external legibility and internal experience.

**Honesty and self-correction as ethical practice.** The academic blog explicitly advocates for admitting error and correcting the record. The personal essay names past "pick-me girl" behavior and internalized misogyny. The newsletter refuses to romanticize the path it recommends. There is a consistent ethical commitment to not pretending — to naming the uncomfortable truth even (especially) when it's about yourself.

**Belonging, community, and who gets to be "default."** Who gets to be unmarked? Whose experience is treated as the norm? The gender essay traces the cost of the masculine default. The newsletter asks who gets to do meaningful work when institutions fail. The academic posts ask who gets to be a "real" scientist. The memoir ideas track a friendship where belonging is negotiated and withdrawn.

**The web as mirror and cage.** The internet is both liberating and constraining across the corpus. It offers validation (browser history gender guesser, forums that use he/him), community (early social media, the trans web), and possibility (the Hook Erika Up Contest), but it also relentlessly demands categorization (gender dropdowns) and enables the worst in people (enshittification, AI sirens). The writer's relationship to the internet is long, deep, and ambivalent.

## Known Weaknesses

*These are observations from reading the drafts, offered for the writer to confirm, reject, or reframe.*

**Sustaining momentum through depth.** The gender essay is powerful but very long, and the final third loses some of the propulsive force of the earlier sections. The abandoned material at the bottom suggests difficulty deciding what to cut. But the issue may be less about length and more about prose-level momentum: when the lyricism and tension are strong enough (as in the early movements of the essay), depth is a virtue. The challenge is maintaining that force all the way through — making the reader want to keep going rather than needing to.

**Completing structures.** The newsletter draft has a thorough Traditional Roles section but trails off into headers for Nontraditional Roles. The gender essay has a similar pattern — the first two-thirds are tightly structured, and the final third loosens. Starting strong and sustaining through the end is an area to watch.

**Transitions.** Shifts between sections or movements can be abrupt, particularly in the longer pieces. The gender essay's move from the Pat sketches to the Yahoo searches to the TA encounter could be smoother. The newsletter jumps from sector overview to job boards without much connective tissue.

**The purely emotional register.** The love letter is effective but very brief, and it's the only piece in the corpus that operates without an intellectual or analytical frame. The writer may be less practiced — or less comfortable — writing in a register where ideas and systems aren't doing some of the structural work.

**Academic hedging in non-academic contexts.** Phrases like "it remains possible that" and "I suspect" are appropriate in the blog posts but occasionally appear where a stronger claim would serve the writing better.

## Aspirations

**Bring more vulnerability and perspective to the professional voice.** The business copy and newsletter currently read as a more guarded person than the one who wrote the gender essay. The writer wants the professional voice to carry more willingness to name personal stakes and to stand by stronger opinions — not by importing the personal essay style wholesale, but by developing a professional register that is identifiably the same person. More humor and style, yes, but the professional voice should find its own version of those things. The Tangle and Zitron influences point toward different models for what this could look like: fair-minded depth with conviction (Tangle), or unapologetic directness with moral clarity (Zitron).

**Develop a distinguishing professional perspective and brand.** The writer wants to write more perspective pieces on AI and data use in the nonprofit space, on working effectively on teams, and on building better relationships. The aspiration is not just to inform but to have a recognizable point of view — to be someone readers seek out for how they think about these topics, not just for the information they provide.

**Get more comfortable with figurative writing and extended metaphors.** The HTTP status code metaphor in the gender essay is the strongest example in the corpus. The writer wants to develop this instinct further, but recognizes that metaphor operates differently across registers. In personal writing, metaphor carries emotional valence and deepens introspection — it's architecture for feeling. In professional writing, metaphor works more as frame and tool — it makes complex ideas graspable and gives the reader a handle. Both are worth developing, but the personal writing is where the deeper, riskier metaphorical work will happen.

**Develop prose beauty and momentum as deliberate craft.** The Padavic-Callaghan, Ishiguro, and McCarthy influences all point toward an aspiration that goes beyond argument and structure: the writer wants the sentences themselves to be better. More musicality, more precision in word choice, more attention to rhythm and sound. But this isn't only aesthetic — it's functional. The "more-ish" quality admired in Keefe and McCarthy comes from prose that generates its own propulsive force. Developing this would reframe the "knowing when to stop" problem: the goal is not to write shorter but to write with enough lyrical momentum that depth feels compelling rather than taxing.

**Learn when to withhold.** The writer's default is to charge directly at self-examination — to name the uncomfortable thing, to push past evasion. This is a genuine strength. But there may be moments, especially in the memoir, where Ishiguro-like restraint would hit harder — where letting structure or metaphor carry meaning that isn't stated outright would create more emotional impact than direct confession. Developing the control to sometimes *not* say the thing, to trust the reader to feel the gap, would expand the writer's range without abandoning the honesty that defines the voice.
